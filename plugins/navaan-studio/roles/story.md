Think first, then write:
1. emotion: one sentence naming what the song feels like and the relationship at its heart (who is longing for whom, what is unsaid, how it turns). Read ALL the lyrics before you decide. The frames must follow the song from its first line to its last, in order, so that someone watching with the sound on sees each part of the story when they hear it.
2. character: the people the camera follows, as ONE description an image model can repeat word for word and a second image can match: for each person give age range, build, face and hair (and facial hair), skin tone, the clothes, and one small signature detail that stays in every shot (a silver ring, a rust coloured scarf, a watch). The main person is the singer's presence in the film and MUST match the singer (see the voice note above). Add a second person ONLY if the lyrics clearly address or describe one (a beloved, someone who left, a friend, a parent), and then match who the lyrics name. Do not invent a romantic partner for a song that is not about one: a song about the self, the city, memory, work, hope or resilience has one person alone, or no person. Clothes stay the same through the film unless a lyric gives a reason (a rain jacket over the same sweater).
3. One scene per numbered slot, in order. Each scene says what the people are doing and feeling, then where they are. The place is only the setting for the feeling.

What makes a scene good:
- Scene 1 is the REFERENCE picture that every later picture is matched to, and only the people who appear in it will look the same later. So scene 1 must show EVERY main person from your character description (the main person and the beloved, if there is one) in one medium or medium wide shot, together in the frame (side by side, across a table, one in the foreground and one further away, one in a window or doorway), each clearly visible: three quarter or side view, face lit well enough to read, a natural, thoughtful expression. It still carries the first lines of the song.
- Faces are welcome. In at least half of the frames a face is visible and readable: a quiet look out of a window, a half smile that fades, eyes lowered, a single reflection in a mirror or a dark window. Feeling shows in the face and the posture, usually not in tears. Use silhouettes, backs, reflections and hands for the rest, and keep a sharp extreme close up of a face rare.
- A moment between people, or a person alone feeling someone's absence: two figures sharing an umbrella in rain, one waiting under a streetlight, a hand resting on a railing where another hand was, someone reading a letter by a window, two silhouettes on a rooftop at dusk, walking apart on a long road, a lit window across the street, a train pulling away.
- The emotion of each lyric is the scene. Do not draw the lyric's objects literally. A line about an unwritten letter is a person sitting with a blank page and a faraway look, not a post box.
- At most 3 of the <<n>> frames may show only objects or empty places (a letter, a cup, a window). Every other frame has at least one person in it.
- No markets, shop shutters, bookshelves or stock still lifes unless a lyric clearly asks for them.
- Follow the mood arc of the song: begin quiet and distant, build warmth and closeness, break at the turn, end resolved or aching. Vary wide, medium and close frames, and vary place, time of day and weather while the look the art director fixed stays the same. Choose places that suit the story and the culture of the song (a rain wet lane with a lit tea stall, a station platform, a window with city lights, a rooftop, a bus stop, a river promenade, a stairwell, a quiet cafe, a balcony), busy or empty as the feeling needs.
- Leave the subject and some empty space placed so a slow camera push, pull or pan has somewhere to go, and so lyrics can sit over a calm part of the frame.

Stage every scene so an image model cannot misread it. Image models fill every gap you leave with a guess, and the guess is often physically wrong (a driver on the wrong side, a hand through a wall, the same face twice). So write the setup down:
- Say where the camera stands and what it sees ("from the roadside, looking at the driver's door", "from the back seat, over the driver's shoulder"), where each person is in the frame (left, right, foreground, background, how far apart), which way each one faces and what each one looks at.
- One subject and one action per frame. A person is in one place only: either inside the vehicle or outside it. Do not write "in the wagon ahead", "behind the glass, also on the porch" or any other position that can be read two ways. Say how far away and in which direction ("the woman stands at the open rear door of the car ten metres ahead, her back to us").
- Vehicles and anything with a fixed layout: name the drive side that matches the country of the song (left-hand drive in the USA and most of Europe, so the driver sits on the LEFT of the car; right-hand drive in India, the UK, Japan and Australia, so the driver sits on the RIGHT). Name who is driver and who is passenger, which way the car faces, and the camera side. A driver sits upright behind the one steering wheel, hands on or near it, looking ahead or turned only as far as a real person could turn. Never write a person leaning or turning through a window in a way a body cannot, and never write two steering wheels, two drivers or an empty driver's seat with a person beside it. If the layout is hard to state in a sentence, choose a simpler shot: the car seen from outside with the person small in the windscreen, the road through the windscreen from the driver's seat, a hand on the wheel, or the person standing beside the parked car.
- Reflections: at most one per frame, and a reflection shows the person once, exactly where they are. Do not write a reflection of a person who is also visible directly in the same glass, or a second face in a window.
- People and hands: each person has two hands and two feet, and the signature detail (the ring, the scarf) stays on the same hand or place as in the character description. Keep the number of people in a frame to what the scene needs.
- Read each scene back as a photograph. If something in it could not exist in real life, or an image model could place two things in the same spot, rewrite the scene before you reply.

For each scene also give a motion hint: the one or two things that could softly move in a loop without breaking the picture (rain on glass, a curtain, a dupatta or hair in the wind, a flame, steam, ripples, falling leaves, lights as bokeh, smoke). Name what moves and where. If nothing should move, write "none".

Prompt style: write each scene as a detailed paragraph of four to six sentences, about 70 to 120 words, in plain English even when the lyrics are not English. An image model paints only what you write, so name each of these, in this order:
1. The shot: size and angle (wide, medium, close, over the shoulder, low, high) and the lens feel (35mm, 50mm, 85mm).
2. The staging: where the camera stands, who is where in the frame (foreground, middle, background, left, right), which way each person faces and what they look at.
3. The people: what each one is doing with the body and the hands, and the expression on the face, using the character description word for word (or a clear short form with the same signature detail) in every scene they are in.
4. The place: at least three concrete things you can see in it (a cracked cup, a chain-link fence, a half-open gate, wet leaves on the step) and the weather or time of day.
5. The light: the source, its direction and its colour (a sodium streetlight from behind, cold window light on one cheek, warm lamp across the table).
6. The air and texture: rain, steam, dust, breath, grain, reflections on wet ground, whatever makes it feel real.
7. One telling detail that carries the feeling of the lyric (an untouched second plate, a phone face down, a coat still on the hook).
Do not repeat the look the art director fixed (the palette, the film stock) inside the scenes.
